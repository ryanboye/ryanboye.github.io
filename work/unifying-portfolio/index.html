Unifying the Product Portfolio

Unity Technologies • Head of Product Design, Grow Solutions

From 40 services to 3 pillars, and how my design team set the direction.

The situation

This is the second chapter of the strategy work I cover in a separate case study. Once we'd crystallized the company's flywheel and gotten everyone speaking the same language, the next problem became obvious: the product portfolio didn't match the strategy.

We had 40 services on the growth side of the business. They were very small SaaS products serving all kinds of different things, and we presented them as one massive sheet of endless things you could do. There was no cohesive strategy holding them together.

If the new strategy was that Unity is one company with one flywheel, the product had to look like that too.

The core decision

The framing that anchored everything: Unity is the product. Everything else is a feature. Once you accept that, the question of "should we have these 40 things" answers itself. You don't sell 40 products. You sell Unity, and Unity has features.

From there, the same exercise we'd done with the company strategy applied to the product portfolio: what are the core pillars? They were already clear once we asked the question — game development, monetization, and user acquisition. With that frame in place, in partnership with our VP, we stopped treating everything as equal-weighted. Three pillars got up-leveled to the top. Everything else became a feature underneath them. Forty to three.

How my design team set the direction

We had a team off-site, and I planned it knowing we needed to rethink how we presented ourselves to customers. Two big focus areas: how we work, and what's the future. The portfolio work came out of the future section.

I challenged the team to imagine the future, knowing everything they knew about the customer and pulling on the customer understanding the design team uniquely has. This is the value designers bring beyond any single siloed product — we see the entire customer journey, and when we put our heads together across the business, we can say what would actually be better for the customer.

I added a constraint: think practically and short-term. We didn't need one-year visions. We needed six-month visions. The business needed real, attainable futures, not aspirational ones.

What came back surprised me. The pitches were strong right out of the gate — strong enough that I forwarded several of them directly to product and engineering leadership. The team had effectively produced the vision deck for the unified product, working from the strategy we'd been refining all year. (And the six-month constraint held: the dashboard we designed at that off-site is launching about six months later. Good on us.)

The second half of this is the part I want to call out: I set the conditions, gave them the strategic context they'd been steeped in, and trusted that the work would surface ideas worth elevating. Then my job was to recognize what was good and get it in front of the people who could act on it.

What happened next

Because we clearly demonstrated what this new future could look like, it immediately resonated. The vision rallied teams and forced the necessary conversations. It was immediately clear that we needed to pivot, and that's what happened. Roadmaps got rewritten. A big dashboard redesign in flight got scrapped. Teams were reassigned. Work people had been pouring themselves into got thrown out.

Change is never easy, and it happens. Sometimes a team gets the flak for forcing it. This time it was my team that got the flak, but everyone knew it was important.

What came out of the pivot was three connected pieces of work, all serving the same strategic purpose: streamline how customers come into the product, and streamline how the product collects the data that powers the model. We unified the product we were launching. We changed the entire texture of how we talked about it — on the homepage, in onboarding, and in the product navigation. I led the marketing site and homepage redesign directly, telling the unified Unity story in the language we'd developed during the strategy work. The new onboarding flow shipped to streamline the customer experience and collect the proprietary signals feeding the ad model. And the new dashboard, built around the unified framing, is launching this month — replacing the one that got scrapped at the off-site.

It ties straight back to the storytelling from the strategy work. It's basically doing what you said you were going to do.

The takeaway

My team sees across product silos because we work across them by nature. We catch the patterns and the contradictions that no single product team is positioned to see. When the company is fragmented, we're often the first ones who can show what unified would look like, because showing is our craft.

That's the bet I keep making, and it's how I think senior design leadership earns its seat.
