Turning a Design Team Into a Coding Team

Unity Technologies • Head of Product Design, Grow Solutions

How I got twelve senior designers, most without any coding background, shipping working code in four weeks.

The situation

I lead a team of 12 senior designers at Unity. Everyone reports directly to me. The company has been flattening, like most companies are right now, and my org is a wide span of people covering a wide range of products. Most of them came up as Figma-first designers with little or no coding background. Only one or two had any coding chops going in. That was the starting point in January.

The call

Over the holidays it became clear to a lot of us in the Valley that the models were getting much more powerful, much faster than expected. Many of us were playing with the new tools and the writing was on the wall. We were in for a big doozy.

In January I gave my team a talking-to. I said something like: this is going to be a bumpy year, this is going to be a radical change to technology and a radical change to how we work, and our roles are going to change a lot. I told them I was going to get them involved early. I told them the tools were going to feel overly tactical and awkward and rough, and the specific stack we'd start on probably wouldn't be the stack we'd end on. I made the bet anyway, and I told them why: if we get in early, you'll understand the technology deeply as it changes, and that capability will outlast any specific tool.

That was the deal we made.

How I ran the transition

I got deep into the tools myself first. I demonstrated some of the work to the team, showing what was actually possible. Then I deputized the two designers who were already further along to showcase what they were building. I paired the rest of the designers with engineers who could unblock them.

That was enough to spin them up. Once people saw a few good examples, they started sharing work with each other and impressing each other. Once that flywheel started turning inside the team, my job shifted to shouting them out and getting their work in front of leadership.

None of this required me to teach them to code. Most of them had never written code before in their lives. They learned because the AI tools made it possible — Cursor, the terminal, Claude. I just got them set up and out of their own way.

Where the team is now

Inside of about four weeks, every designer on the team was committing to Git and pushing staging links. Twelve senior designers, most of whom had never opened a terminal, shipping working prototypes into the actual codebase.

The tangible business impact is in how product teams work now. Before this, fleshing out a design and getting alignment on it was a multi-week thing. Designers would spec it, product would review it, engineering would weigh in, iterations would happen async, and weeks would go by before anything got tested with real users.

Now the same product teams get in a room together, live-prototype, and run four or five tested iterations in a week. Designers are coding and managing agents. The whole loop got faster by an order of magnitude, and the work got better because more iterations means more learning.

The bet

The thing I told the team in January is the thing I still believe now. The specific tools are going to keep changing — they already have. What outlasts the tools is the capability. Designers who understand the technology deeply, who can prototype directly into the codebase, who can manage agents and ship working artifacts, are going to be the ones who matter as the discipline changes.

Most design orgs are still figuring out whether to take this seriously. I made the call early, made the bet explicitly with my team, and got 12 senior designers shipping code in a month. That's the kind of leadership move I think this moment in the industry actually requires.
