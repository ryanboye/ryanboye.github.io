Crystallizing Unity's Strategy

Unity Technologies • Head of Product Design, Grow Solutions

How a fuzzy company narrative became the language we used to talk about ourselves — and the speech the SVP gave at Unite.

The situation

To understand this story you have to understand where Unity was. The stock had been down something like 90% from its peak. We'd been through years of compounding problems, two CEO transitions, restructuring, layoffs, and a real crisis of identity about what kind of company we even were. Our new permanent CEO was about eight months into the seat, getting his arms around the business and starting to point us toward a turnaround. I'd been a thought leader through that whole arc, working on strategy and storytelling for years before this particular chapter. This case study is one chapter in a longer turnaround story, but it's the chapter where the work crystallized.

The specific bottleneck was narrative. Unity had two business units, gaming and ads, that had been bolted together for years without a coherent story about why they belonged in the same company. People hated ads. People didn't understand why ads was in here with gaming. It didn't make sense to them, and frankly it didn't fully make sense to a lot of us internally either. Without that story, every team was just doing their thing because their thing existed. They weren't thinking about how their thing fit into anyone else's thing. The whole company was pushing in a hundred slightly different directions.

How I got involved

Will Goldstone, the Senior Director of Design on the gaming side, and I started working with the CEO on some of his board slides, trying to articulate why the two business units fit together. That work caught on. The framing we developed started to permeate other parts of the company, and I got pulled into my side, Grow Solutions, to do the same thing one level down.

So the arc was: start at the company level with the CEO, get the framing right there, then take that framing and crystallize the strategy for the entire growth side of the business — including how we positioned Vector, our new ad network, as a new system rather than a redo of the old failing one.

What we actually did

The thing I'm best at, and the thing I leaned on hardest here, is taking complicated thinking and crystallizing it down to something that's bare-bones simple. That's the move. Not making it look pretty, not making it sound smart. Making it so simple that anyone in the company could read it and immediately know which part of the picture they were working on.

We landed on a flywheel. The engine generates proprietary signals. Those signals feed the ad algorithm. The ads make money. The money funds R&D. R&D builds things developers love. More developers means more games, which means more data feeding the engine. Once you could see it laid out like that, it was obvious where every team fit. It was also obvious which things weren't generating data, weren't using data, and probably shouldn't exist.

That flywheel, and the language around it, became the spine of decks, narrative documents, go-to-market materials, and marketing site copy across the year.

Where it ended up

Once everyone was speaking the same language, prioritization got a lot easier. People could see whether their work was creating data or using data, and the conversations about what to invest in versus what to sunset got dramatically less political. That work directly fed the product portfolio pivot we ran later in the year, which I cover in a separate case study.

I ended up being the go-to growth voice in C-suite strategy sessions for the rest of the year. The culmination was Unite, our annual customer conference. I wrote the SVP's keynote speech outright and informed a portion of another VP's keynote section. The messaging I'd been refining all year became the official messaging the company used to tell its story to thousands of customers in person and many more dialing in.

The leadership lesson

The thing I want to be honest about: the flywheel isn't impressive on its own. Lots of people can draw a flywheel. What's hard is being in the room with a CEO and an SVP who are mid-thought, listening carefully enough to hear the actual shape of what they're trying to say, and then handing it back to them in a form that's so clean they immediately recognize it as theirs.

That's the muscle I've been building my whole career, and it's the muscle I think a senior design leader actually needs. Not taste. Not Figma fluency. The ability to walk into a strategically incoherent situation and make it legible — first to the executives, then to the company, then to the customer. I helped Unity do that during one of the hardest stretches in its history, and the language we developed in those rooms is the language the company still uses today.
